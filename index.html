Design a bridging mechanism for an X thread

In week 2, fact-checks were used as entry points into speech grey zones. In week 3, these were described: how fact-checkers and community noters disagreed, what commenters said, and what they agreed and disagreed on. This week's assignment moves from describing the conflict to intervening in it.

The intervention is called bridging: a mechanism for facilitating understanding — the possibility that users who make conflicting statements have the information they need to understand and engage with each other. Bridging is a framework, not a single technique: every operationalisation names a bridging goal (what would count as more understanding), a relation metric (what measurable property of a comment stands in for it) and an attention allocator (what the platform does with it — here, how it orders the comments).

Your assignment: take one disputed X thread, define three bridging metrics, code every comment of the thread by them with a chatbot, and reorder the thread so that comments with bridging properties come first. X orders threads by engagement; in this assignment the thread is ordered by your metrics, and the result is presented in class.

Six steps

  1. Pick a post

    Choose one of the disputed posts — where the fact-checker and the community notes reach opposite conclusions — and download its thread as a CSV. Each row is the post, the fact-check, a community note or a reply; the Statement column says which statements each row makes.

  2. Close read by statement

    Read the thread statement by statement: which statements the majority makes, which the minority makes, and where users misunderstand, misrepresent or ignore one another.

  3. Define your bridging metrics

    Three metrics, each with a name, what it captures, and a coding rule precise enough that someone else — or a chatbot — would code every comment the same way. For example: a comment has breadth if it makes two or more different statements.

  4. Code the thread with a chatbot

    The site turns your metrics into a prompt. Give it, with your CSV, to a chatbot: it adds a yes/no column for each metric. Check its coding against your own on 20 comments.

  5. Upload the coded dataset

    Upload the CSV the chatbot returned (in several parts, if it split it).

  6. Order the thread

    Decide how the thread is ordered: by engagement, chronologically, or by your metrics — choosing a metric moves every comment that has it to the top. Combine them: for example metric A first, then the most liked. As on X, community notes and replies are shown and ordered separately. The three metrics can be compared side by side.

How your work is saved

Each user works separately. Files you open, text you write and codes you add are not sent anywhere, and nothing you do changes the site for other users.

  • Automatically, in your browser. Your metrics, uploads and orders are kept in the browser you use, and remain available on the same computer under Your saved results.
  • As a result file. On the last page, Download your result file saves everything — your coded thread, your metrics and your order — in one file. It can be opened on the same page on any other computer, including the one in the classroom.
  • As a presentation. Download a presentation page saves a page that opens in any browser, including offline, and shows your metrics and your reordered thread.

Work in groups of three or four, one post per group. Each of you can follow the steps on your own computer; share files within the group.
